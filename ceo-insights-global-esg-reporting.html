Compliance-Led ESG Reporting - A Global Perspective.

Executive Headline
We have analysed 20 jurisdictions covering the G20 and major economic blocs. ESG reporting is now mandatory or in phased implementation across every one of them. ISSB is becoming the de facto global baseline. The question is no longer whether disclosure is required - it is whether your reporting infrastructure can serve multiple jurisdictions at once.

The Shift - What Is Changing

ESG disclosure has crossed a threshold. Every jurisdiction in our analysis - from the EU to India to California - now has a mandatory or formally adopted ESG reporting regime. The era of voluntary disclosure is over as a sufficient response. What is emerging in its place is a compliance-led global reporting environment where the underlying frameworks are converging on ISSB, the assurance expectations are rising, and the cost of maintaining separate reporting stacks for each jurisdiction is becoming unsustainable.

Why It Matters - Business Impact
🌍
No safe harbour remains
Every G20 jurisdiction now has ESG reporting obligations. Multinationals operating across the EU, US, UK, Australia, Japan, and India face overlapping requirements that cannot be managed as separate compliance projects without significant duplication.
🔗
ISSB is winning
13 of 20 jurisdictions reference ISSB standards. Australia, Canada, Japan, South Africa, Singapore, and the UK are all adopting ISSB-aligned frameworks. Companies that build to ISSB S1 and S2 will cover the majority of their global compliance requirements from a single baseline.
⚡
2025-2026 is the activation window
The majority of phased regimes - Australia, Canada, US, California, Korea, China - move into first reporting cycles in 2025 and 2026. For multinationals, this is not a future planning item. The data collection and assurance infrastructure needs to exist now.

  • ›Does our Scope 3 data quality meet the supplier disclosure expectations of our largest customers in EU and UK regulated markets?
💡
Key Takeaway
The global ESG reporting landscape has converged faster than most companies planned for. ISSB is the emerging spine. CSRD is the most demanding layer. Companies that build once to the highest applicable standard - and use that infrastructure globally - will spend less and disclose more than those managing each jurisdiction separately.

ISSB as global baseline
Australia, Canada, Japan, South Africa, Singapore, UK, Nigeria, and others are all adopting ISSB-aligned local standards. Building to ISSB S1 and S2 covers the majority of climate disclosure requirements globally from a single data architecture.
TCFD as the foundation
TCFD underpins most climate-specific disclosure requirements. It is now embedded in ISSB S2, ESRS E1, and numerous national frameworks. TCFD alignment is effectively a prerequisite for any climate disclosure regime, not a voluntary add-on.
GRI for social and governance
GRI remains widely referenced for social and governance disclosures, particularly where ISSB S1 broader sustainability topics are in scope. Eight jurisdictions reference GRI alongside ISSB or TCFD.
ESRS as EU-specific layer
ESRS is mandatory for CSRD-in-scope entities only. Outside the EU, it is referenced for interoperability mapping. Companies that build to ESRS will find ISSB coverage largely included - but the reverse is not true.

Section 4 Foresight - What Changes
Now EU Wave 1 companies are reporting under CSRD. India, South Africa, Brazil, and several G20 markets already have established reporting regimes. Japan's SSBJ standards are producing first ISSB-aligned reports. The compliance baseline is set - the question is quality, assurance, and integration.
12 months Australia, Canada, South Korea, China, California, and New York all enter first formal reporting cycles in FY2025-26. The SEC climate rules, though scaled back, apply to large accelerated filers. CSRD extends to Wave 2 mid-size companies. The volume of companies in scope globally more than doubles.
3 to 5 years ISSB-aligned frameworks will be operational in the majority of G20 jurisdictions. Cross-border data comparability will improve as ISSB becomes the common language. Assurance expectations will tighten globally following the CSRD limited assurance model. Companies that have not built audit-ready ESG data infrastructure will face simultaneous pressure across multiple jurisdictions.
Section 5 Inaction - Consequences
Companies that continue to treat ESG reporting as a voluntary or jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction exercise will find the cost of remediation growing as the number of active regimes multiplies. The window for building shared infrastructure - one data model serving ISSB, CSRD, and national adaptations simultaneously - is open now. Multinationals that miss this window will rebuild separately for each jurisdiction under time pressure and at significantly higher cost. The assurance expectations embedded in CSRD will increasingly migrate to other jurisdictions. Companies without audit-ready ESG data will face qualified opinions in multiple markets simultaneously.
